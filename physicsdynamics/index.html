Section 01

Newton's three laws

Everything in this module traces back to these three statements. Learn them precisely, not vaguely, because examiners will test the exact wording as much as the calculations.

The laws describe how forces cause (or don't cause) changes in motion. The key word throughout is net force — the vector sum of all forces acting on an object. Single forces in isolation rarely tell the full story.

1st Law
Inertia
An object stays at rest, or moves in a straight line at constant speed, unless acted on by a non-zero net force.
2nd Law
F = ma
The net force on an object equals its mass times acceleration. Larger force or smaller mass means larger acceleration.
3rd Law
Action-Reaction
For every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force. They act on different objects and never cancel each other.
Newton's Second Law $$\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m\vec{a} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m}$$
Common Mistake Newton's 3rd Law pairs act on different objects, so they never cancel. Students often confuse "balanced forces" (equal and opposite forces on the same object, making net force zero) with action-reaction pairs (forces on two different objects). These are completely different situations.
Key Concept: Inertia Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes to its state of motion. It is measured by mass. A more massive object requires a larger net force to produce the same acceleration. Inertia is a property of matter, not a force.

Q · 01
A book sits perfectly still on a table. What is true about the forces on the book?
Correct. Newton's 1st Law: the book is at rest, so net force = 0. The downward weight force is balanced exactly by the upward normal reaction force from the table. Both forces are real and equal in magnitude.
Q · 02
A 5.0 kg block accelerates at $4.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ to the right. The net force on the block is:
Correct. $F_{\text{net}} = ma = 5.0 \times 4.0 = 20\ \text{N}$. Direction matters: net force is always in the direction of acceleration.
Q · 03
You push a wall with 80 N. By Newton's Third Law, the reaction force is:
Correct. The wall doesn't moving is irrelevant to the force magnitude. 3rd Law pairs are always equal in magnitude. The reaction force acts on you, not the wall — that's why it doesn't cancel your push force.
Section 02

Forces & friction

Friction is the force that opposes the relative motion (or attempted motion) between surfaces. It is proportional to the normal force — not the contact area.

The frictional force depends on two things: how hard the surfaces press together (the normal force $F_N$) and how rough the surfaces are (the coefficient of friction $\mu$). That's it. Surface area does not appear in the formula.

Friction $$f = \mu F_N$$
Static friction
$f_s \leq \mu_s F_N$

Acts when the surfaces are not sliding. Adjusts itself to match the applied force up to a maximum. The object does not move while $F_{\text{applied}} \leq \mu_s F_N$.

Kinetic friction
$f_k = \mu_k F_N$

Acts when surfaces are sliding. Constant in magnitude (for a given pair of surfaces). Always $\mu_k < \mu_s$, which is why it is easier to keep something sliding than to start it sliding.

Classic Trap If an object is at rest and the applied force is less than the maximum static friction, the actual friction force equals the applied force — not $\mu_s F_N$. The formula $f = \mu_s F_N$ gives the maximum before sliding begins. Applying it blindly is one of the most common errors on the exam.

A 10 kg box is pulled across a rough floor by a horizontal force of 60 N. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.35. Find the acceleration.

1
Normal force: $F_N = mg = 10 \times 9.8 = 98\ \text{N}$
2
Friction force: $f_k = \mu_k F_N = 0.35 \times 98 = 34.3\ \text{N}$
3
Net force: $F_{\text{net}} = 60 - 34.3 = 25.7\ \text{N}$
4
Acceleration: $a = \dfrac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \dfrac{25.7}{10} = 2.57\ \text{m s}^{-2}$

Q · 04
A 10 kg crate is at rest. Applied force = 40 N. $\mu_s = 0.50$. The frictional force acting on the crate is:
Tricky one. Max static friction = $0.50 \times 10 \times 9.8 = 49\ \text{N}$. Since 40 N < 49 N, the crate does not move. Static friction adjusts to exactly balance the applied force: $f_s = 40\ \text{N}$. You only use $\mu_s F_N$ when the object is on the verge of slipping.
Q · 05
Which of these does NOT affect the kinetic friction force between two surfaces?
Correct. $f_k = \mu_k F_N$. Area does not appear in the formula and does not affect friction. This is counterintuitive but true for most everyday surfaces.
Q · 06
A rope pulls a 15 kg box at 25° above horizontal with a tension of 80 N. What is the normal force on the box?
Correct. The rope's vertical component partially supports the box: $F_N = mg - T\sin25° = 15(9.8) - 80(0.423) = 147 - 33.8 \approx 113\ \text{N}$. An angled pull always reduces the normal force, which also reduces friction — that's why you pull at an angle when dragging heavy objects.
Section 03

Inclined planes

A slope is just Newton's 2nd Law with the weight vector split into two components: one along the slope, one perpendicular to it.

The trick with inclined planes is choosing your coordinate system wisely. Tilt the axes so that $x$ runs along the slope and $y$ runs perpendicular to it. Weight $mg$ then splits into:

  • Parallel to slope: $F_\parallel = mg\sin\theta$ (causes acceleration along the slope)
  • Perpendicular to slope: $F_\perp = mg\cos\theta$ (equals the normal force on a frictionless slope)
On a slope (tilted axes) $$F_N = mg\cos\theta \qquad a = g\sin\theta \text{ (frictionless)}$$
Finding the friction angle On a slope with friction, the object is on the verge of sliding when $mg\sin\theta = \mu mg\cos\theta$. The masses cancel, giving $\tan\theta = \mu$. So $\theta = \arctan(\mu)$ — the angle at which the object just starts to slide is independent of mass. This is called the friction angle.

A 4.0 kg block slides down a frictionless 35° incline. Find its acceleration and the normal force.

1
Normal force: $F_N = mg\cos35° = 4.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.819 = 32.1\ \text{N}$
2
Net force along slope: $F_\parallel = mg\sin35° = 4.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.574 = 22.5\ \text{N}$
3
Acceleration: $a = g\sin35° = 9.8 \times 0.574 = 5.62\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ (down the slope)
Check: Inclined Planes

Q · 07
A 5 kg block on a frictionless 30° incline. Its acceleration is:
Correct. $a = g\sin30° = 9.8 \times 0.5 = 4.9\ \text{m s}^{-2}$. The mass cancels entirely. Note the block doesn't fall at full $g$ because the slope redirects part of the gravitational force into the normal force.
Q · 08
A 50 kg crate just starts to slide on a slope when the angle reaches 30°. What is $\mu_s$?
Correct. At the point of slipping: $\tan\theta = \mu_s$, so $\mu_s = \tan30° \approx 0.577$. Notice the mass of the crate is completely irrelevant — it cancels out of both sides of the equation.
Section 04

Work, energy & power

Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed. This is the most powerful idea in the module — it lets you solve problems that would be nightmarish with forces alone.

The Energy Toolkit $$W = Fs\cos\theta \qquad K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \qquad \Delta U = mg\Delta h$$ $$P = \frac{\Delta E}{\Delta t} = Fv\cos\theta$$
Conservation of Mechanical Energy If there are no dissipative forces (friction, air resistance), the total mechanical energy $E = K + U$ is constant. Mathematically: $K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f$. This means you can find velocities and heights without knowing time or acceleration.
Common Mistake The $\theta$ in $W = Fs\cos\theta$ is the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector — not the slope angle or any other angle in the problem. Sketch a free body diagram and identify this angle explicitly before substituting.
Conservation of Mechanical Energy
Watch a pendulum swing. Kinetic and potential energy trade off, but their sum stays constant.
KE (J) 0.00
PE (J) 4.90
Total E (J) 4.90

At the extremes: all PE, zero KE. At the bottom: all KE, zero PE. The total stays constant — that's conservation of mechanical energy.

A 2.0 kg ball is released from rest at a height of 5.0 m on a frictionless ramp. What is its speed at the bottom?

1
Set up energy conservation: $K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f$. At rest at top: $K_i = 0$. At bottom: $U_f = 0$.
2
Simplify: $mgΔh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. The mass cancels: $gΔh = \tfrac{1}{2}v^2$
3
Solve: $v = \sqrt{2g\Delta h} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 5.0} = \sqrt{98} \approx 9.90\ \text{m s}^{-1}$
4
Note: The mass of 2.0 kg was not needed. Any mass dropped from 5.0 m reaches the same speed (on a frictionless surface).
Check: Energy and Power

Q · 09
A 3 kg ball has initial KE of 60 J at ground level and is thrown straight up. How high does it rise? (Use $g = 9.8$)
Correct. $\Delta U = KE_i \Rightarrow mgh = 60 \Rightarrow h = 60/(3 \times 9.8) = 2.04\ \text{m}$. Use energy conservation — you don't need initial speed or time.
Q · 10
A force of 60 N is applied at 45° above horizontal for a displacement of 10 m. Work done is:
Correct. $W = Fs\cos\theta = 60 \times 10 \times \cos45° = 600 \times 0.707 \approx 424\ \text{J}$. Only the horizontal component of force does work on horizontal displacement.
Q · 11
A 1000 kg car travels at constant 20 m/s against 500 N of air resistance. Engine power required:
Correct. Constant speed means net force = 0, so engine force = drag = 500 N. $P = Fv = 500 \times 20 = 10\,000\ \text{W} = 10\ \text{kW}$.
Q · 12
A ball (5 kg) slides down a ramp from height 8 m and onto a rough floor. It stops after 10 m on the floor. The average friction force on the floor is:
Correct. KE at bottom of ramp = $mgh = 5 \times 9.8 \times 8 = 392\ \text{J}$. Friction does work $W = f \times d = f \times 10$. Setting equal: $f = 392/10 = 39.2\ \text{N}$. Work-energy theorem is the slick approach here.
Section 05

Momentum & collisions

Momentum is always conserved in a closed system. This is what makes it so powerful: you can analyse collisions without needing to know the forces involved or the collision duration.

Momentum and Conservation $$p = mv \qquad \sum m\vec{v}_{\text{before}} = \sum m\vec{v}_{\text{after}}$$
Elastic collision
KE is conserved

Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. Objects bounce off each other and no energy is lost to heat, sound or deformation. Rare in practice — billiard balls are close.

Inelastic collision
KE is NOT conserved

Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is converted to other forms (heat, sound, deformation). In a perfectly inelastic collision the objects stick together. This is the most common type.

Direction is Critical Momentum is a vector. Before substituting, define a positive direction (usually "to the right"). Any object moving left gets a negative velocity. Forgetting signs is responsible for most wrong answers in collision problems.
Conservation of Momentum
Two trolleys collide. Total momentum before equals total momentum after, regardless of collision type.
p of A (kg·m/s) 6.00
p of B (kg·m/s) -2.00
Total p (kg·m/s) 4.00

A: 2 kg at +3 m/s (rightward). B: 2 kg at -1 m/s (leftward). Total momentum = 4.00 kg m/s throughout — watch the animation to see it conserved after collision.

A 1500 kg car moving east at 20 m/s collides with a 1000 kg ute moving west at 15 m/s. They lock together. Find the velocity of the wreckage.

1
Define positive direction: Let east = positive. Ute's velocity = $-15\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.
2
Total initial momentum: $p_i = 1500(20) + 1000(-15) = 30\,000 - 15\,000 = 15\,000\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}$ east
3
Conservation: $p_f = (m_1 + m_2)v_f = 2500 \cdot v_f$
4
Solve: $v_f = 15\,000 / 2500 = 6.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ east
Check: Momentum

Q · 13
Object A (2 kg, 6 m/s right) collides with stationary B (4 kg). They stick together. Final velocity:
Correct. $m_1 v_1 = (m_1+m_2)v_f \Rightarrow 2 \times 6 = 6 \times v_f \Rightarrow v_f = 2.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Notice the final velocity is much less than initial — kinetic energy was lost (inelastic).
Q · 14
Enzo (50 kg) and Jake (80 kg) stand at rest on a frictionless ice rink. They push off each other. Enzo moves at 3.0 m/s. Jake's speed is:
Correct. Initial momentum = 0. So $50 \times 3 + 80 \times v_J = 0 \Rightarrow v_J = -150/80 = -1.875\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Jake moves in the opposite direction, with speed 1.88 m/s. Heavier means slower, not stationary.
Q · 15
In an inelastic collision, which of the following is always true?
Correct. Momentum is always conserved in any collision in a closed system. Kinetic energy is only conserved in elastic collisions. Objects sticking together is a special case (perfectly inelastic). Total energy is never destroyed — it converts to heat and sound.
Section 06

Impulse

Impulse connects force and time to the change in momentum. It explains why a long, soft landing is safer than a short, hard one — and why airbags save lives.

Impulse-Momentum Theorem $$\text{Impulse} = \Delta p = F_{\text{net}}\,\Delta t = m\Delta v$$
Graphical Interpretation On a force-time graph, the impulse equals the area under the curve. A tall, narrow spike (short hard collision) and a short, wide curve (long soft collision) can have identical areas — identical impulses — but very different peak forces. This is the physics of crumple zones and airbags.
Force vs Time: Two Collisions
Same impulse (same area), but the longer collision has a much smaller peak force.
Peak Force A HIGH
Peak Force B LOW
Impulse (both) Same

Airbags extend the collision time Δt. Since $F = \Delta p / \Delta t$, a larger Δt means a smaller force on the passenger for the same change in momentum (stopping from 60 km/h to 0).

A 0.060 kg tennis ball traveling at 25 m/s is struck by a racquet and returns at 30 m/s in the opposite direction. Contact time is 5.0 ms. Find the average force on the ball.

1
Define positive direction: Let the initial direction = positive. So $v_i = +25\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ and $v_f = -30\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.
2
Change in momentum: $\Delta p = m(v_f - v_i) = 0.060(-30 - 25) = 0.060 \times (-55) = -3.3\ \text{kg m s}^{-1}$
3
Average force: $F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{-3.3}{0.0050} = -660\ \text{N}$
4
Interpret: Magnitude = 660 N, directed opposite to the ball's initial motion (the racquet pushes the ball back).
Check: Impulse
0 / 2
Q · 16
A 0.10 kg ball hits a wall at 10 m/s and bounces straight back at 8 m/s. The magnitude of the impulse is:
Correct. Take initial direction as positive: $v_i = +10$, $v_f = -8$. $J = m\Delta v = 0.10 \times (-8 - 10) = 0.10 \times (-18) = -1.8\ \text{N·s}$. Magnitude = 1.8 N·s. Don't just subtract the magnitudes — the direction reversal matters.
Q · 17
The fundamental SI unit of impulse is:
Correct. Impulse = change in momentum. Momentum has units kg·m·s-1. While N·s is also correct (and equivalent), the fundamental SI unit uses base units only: kg, m, s.
